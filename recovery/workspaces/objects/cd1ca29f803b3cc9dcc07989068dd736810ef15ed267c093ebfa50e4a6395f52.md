# exec-report: adversary-forced winning frontier

**Worktree:** `soring301-exec-adversary-forced-frontier-20261117`, HEAD `6a96398`, clean tree.
**Question (all n):** Is there a partial order that every height-`k` comparison strategy for `n` keys must encounter and that remains winning at budget `k`, or are the `2^{Ω(n log² n)}` winning-frontier states merely reachable in some optimal strategy?
**Result:** No all-`n` adversary forcing of a rectangular-chain winning state is obtained. The family's defining cross-level comparisons are optional for a sorter, so the winning-frontier count is not by itself an adversary lower bound. The complementary small-`n` evidence is avoidance (the family is absent from all optimal-play reachable states at the first nontrivial size).

## 1. Definitions

Let `k` be a comparison budget and let a **state** be the transitively closed partial order `P` induced by the comparisons made so far after `t` outcomes, with `F(P)` the minimax remaining comparisons from `P`.

- **Reachable winning state:** there exists a height-`k` sorting strategy and a root-to-leaf path such that after `t` comparisons the induced poset is `P` and `F(P) ≤ k-t`. (This is the winning-frontier family: reachable plus sortable within the remaining budget.)
- **Forced winning state:** for *every* height-`k` sorting strategy there is a root-to-leaf path whose state after some `t` comparisons is isomorphic to `P` and satisfies `F(P) ≤ k-t`. Equivalently, the adversary has a strategy in the comparison game that steers any sorter into `P` while keeping the budget nonnegative.
- **Height-`k` sorter:** a deterministic adaptive decision tree of depth `≤ k` that sorts all `n!` inputs.

The winning-frontier construction gives `2^{(1/4-o(1)) n log² n}` pairwise nonisomorphic states satisfying reachability and winningness. It does not establish forcing: those states are (a)-(c)-viable states in the sense of the optimal-viable-state-counting front, not necessarily states on an optimal (ancestor-optimal) path.

## 2. Adversary construction attempts

**Attempt A — force the rectangular chain exactly.** The rectangular family has `s` initial chains of length `L` (`t₀=s(L-1)` comparisons) plus a selected set of `m` extra edges `(i,x)<(i+1,y)` with `x≠y`. To end in a member of the family, the sorter must make those `t₀+m` comparisons. The adversary can answer consistently with any hidden total order extending a target `P_A`, but it cannot make the sorter compare a pair the sorter does not choose. Thus exact forcing of a member with `m>0` would require every height-`k` sorter to make those specific cross-level comparisons. No such necessity is established; a sorter may omit them entirely.

**Attempt B — force some member of the family.** The adversary can choose a hidden order so that whenever a cross-level pair is compared, the outcome follows a selected subset. But if a sorter never compares cross-level pairs, its state has `m=0` selected edges and, for `m>0`, is not in the family. So forcing "some member" reduces to showing that every height-`k` sorter must use at least one selected cross-level comparison in the exact required combination. The small-`n` evidence below goes the other way.

**Attempt C — force by counting.** A single height-`k` (optimal) strategy has at most `n!·k = 2^{O(n log n)}` distinct reachable states, while the family has `2^{Ω(n log² n)}` members. This rules out one strategy containing the whole family, but it does not by itself force a common state. The decisive check is optimal-play reachability, where the family is absent at the first nontrivial size.

## 3. Small-`n` experiments

- `n=6`, `S(6)=10`, rectangular parameters `s=2,L=3`: `t₀=4`, `B=6`, `m=0`. The family reduces to the base chain of two 3-chains. The union of all optimal-play reachable states has `65,551` labelled states; intersection with this family (up to isomorphism) is `0`. A single canonical optimal policy has `1,439` states and also avoids it.
- `n=5`: `700` labelled states (`10` unlabelled) satisfy budget-root-reachability, information admissibility, and local viability but are not on any height-`S(5)=7` optimal strategy. Thus winningness alone does not imply optimal-play reachability.
- `n=4`: `m=-1`, so no winning rectangular state exists at the exact budget.

## 4. Labeled claims

- **[definition]** Forced vs reachable winning states (§1).
- **[deduction]** Rectangular family members with `m>0` contain selected cross-level edges. An adversary cannot force a comparison that the sorter does not make, so exact forcing of such a state is not obtained. The family is therefore not shown to be forced against every height-`k` sorter.
- **[experiment]** At `n=6`, the base chain is absent from all `65,551` optimal-play reachable states.
- **[conjecture]** A height-`S(n)` strategy avoiding the rectangular family exists for all `n`, because its defining comparisons are optional and small-`n` canonical optimal strategies avoid the family. An explicit all-`n` construction is not given here.
- **[barrier consequence]** The winning-frontier count remains a barrier for paradigms that enumerate (a)-(c)-viable states, but it is not an adversary lower bound for arbitrary algorithms unless forcing is proved.

## 5. Stop condition

No all-`n` adversary forcing a rectangular-chain winning state is obtained. The attempts fail because the family's extra comparisons are sorter-controlled. The front therefore stops on the no-forcing side: the rectangular-chain winning states are reachable in some strategies but are not established as forced, and small-`n` evidence supports avoidance. An explicit all-`n` height-`k` strategy avoiding the whole family remains open.
