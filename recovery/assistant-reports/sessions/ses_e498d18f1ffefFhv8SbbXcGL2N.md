**Resolved by a simpler counting argument; no typical-module claim is needed.**

An interval-module reversal is a permutation of the elements of a poset. Relabellings already include every permutation in \(S_n\). Therefore, under the equivalence relation generated by relabelling *and* interval-module reversals, two posets are equivalent exactly when they are isomorphic. Adding the reversals does not enlarge the relabelling orbits.

Let \(P_n\) be the number of labelled \(n\)-posets and \(U_n\) the number of equivalence classes. Each class contains at most \(n!\) labelled posets, so
\[
P_n/n! \le U_n \le P_n.
\]
Using the KR asymptotic recorded in `docs/research-encoding-consequences.md`, \(\log_2 P_n=n^2/4+3n/2+O(\log n)\), and \(\log_2(n!)=O(n\log n)=o(n^2)\). Hence
\[
\log_2 U_n=n^2/4+o(n^2),
\]
so the quotient still has \(2^{n^2/4+o(n^2)}\) classes.

## Correction: the previous conclusion was invalid

The earlier claim that relabelling absorbs interval reversals is wrong. An interval reversal changes the order relation; it does **not** produce an isomorphic copy. Hence it can merge distinct isomorphism classes, and the bound \(C_n \ge P_n/n!\) does not follow.

The proposed KR typical-module lemma is also false as stated. In the standard three-layer KR model \(L_0<L_1<L_2\), the sets
\[
L_0,\quad L_1,\quad L_2,\quad L_0\cup L_1,\quad L_1\cup L_2
\]
are interval modules (each has all outside elements uniformly above or below). Several are nontrivial. So one should not build the proof on “typical posets have no nontrivial interval modules.”

## Correct result: the leading exponent is unchanged

Let \(C_n\) be the number of equivalence classes of labelled \(n\)-posets under relabelling and interval-module reversals.

### Lemma 1 (reversal preserves the comparability graph)

If \(S\) is an interval module of \(P\), then \(\sigma_S(P)\) has the same comparability graph as \(P\).

Indeed, an interval module gives an ordinal sum
\[
P=A\oplus S\oplus B,
\]
with every element of \(A\) below every element of \(S\), and every element of \(S\) below every element of \(B\). Reversing \(S\) only reverses orientations inside \(S\). It preserves which pairs are comparable, both inside \(S\) and across the three blocks. Hence the comparability graph is unchanged.

### Lemma 2 (a graph has at most \(n!\) transitive orientations)

For any graph \(G\) on \(n\) vertices, the number of partial orders whose comparability graph is exactly \(G\) is at most \(n!\).

Proof by induction on \(n\). For a nonempty poset \(P\), choose a minimal element \(v\). Then \(P-v\) is a poset with comparability graph \(G-v\). For a fixed \(v\), the map
\[
P\longmapsto P-v
\]
is injective: minimality of \(v\) forces every edge incident to \(v\) to be oriented outward from \(v\). Therefore
\[
t(G)\le \sum_{v\in V(G)} t(G-v)\le n\cdot (n-1)!=n!.
\]
The bound is tight for the complete graph \(K_n\), whose transitive orientations are exactly the \(n!\) total orders.

### Lemma 3 (each quotient class has at most \((n!)^2\) labelled posets)

A relabelling and a reversal commute up to conjugating the module:
\[
L_\pi R_S=R_{\pi(S)}L_\pi .
\]
Thus every word in relabellings and interval reversals can be rewritten as a single initial relabelling \(\pi\), followed by a sequence of interval reversals.

Fix \(P\) and let \(G\) be its comparability graph. After relabelling by \(\pi\), the graph is \(\pi(G)\). By Lemma 1, every subsequent interval reversal preserves that graph. By Lemma 2, for each fixed \(\pi\), there are at most \(n!\) possible resulting posets. There are at most \(n!\) relabellings \(\pi\). Hence
\[
|\,[P]\,|\le (n!)^2.
\]

### Lower bound

Use the explicit height-two family: split \([n]\) into \(A,B\) with \(|A|=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), \(|B|=\lceil n/2\rceil\). For every bipartite graph \(H\subseteq A\times B\), impose \(a<b\) exactly for the edges of \(H\), with no other relations. This is a poset, and distinct \(H\) give distinct labelled posets. Hence
\[
P_n\ge 2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\lceil n/2\rceil}
=2^{n^2/4-O(1)}.
\]
Therefore
\[
C_n\ge \frac{P_n}{(n!)^2}
\ge 2^{\,n^2/4-2\log_2(n!)-O(1)}
=2^{\,n^2/4-o(n^2)}.
\]
In particular \(C_n=2^{\Omega(n^2)}\).

### Upper bound

Trivially \(C_n\le P_n\). The Kleitman–Rothschild enumeration, as recorded in `docs/research-encoding-consequences.md` Lemma 1.4 and sourced there, gives
\[
\log_2 P_n=\frac{n^2}{4}+\frac{3n}{2}+O(\log n)
=\frac{n^2}{4}+o(n^2).
\]
Thus
\[
C_n\le 2^{\,n^2/4+o(n^2)}.
\]

Combining,
\[
\boxed{\;C_n=2^{\,n^2/4+o(n^2)}\;}
\]

So the interval-module-reversal quotient reduces the full state table by at most a factor
\[
(n!)^2=2^{O(n\log n)}=2^{o(n^2)},
\]
which cannot change the leading exponent.

This is a statement about the **global memoized full-state quotient only**. It is not a time lower bound for selective algorithms, not a bound on root-optimal winning-frontier quotients, and not a barrier for other state summaries.

The finite counts \(2045\to915\) at \(n=7\) and \(16999\to7573\) at \(n=8\) are consistent with this: the observed factor is about \(2.2\), far below the allowed \(2^{o(n^2)}\). They are illustration only, not part of the proof.
