## Precise question

Is `{(n,s) : S(n) <= L(n)+s}` decidable in `f(s)*poly(n)` time (FPT in the
surplus `s = k - L(n)`)? Equivalently, is the function `e(n) = S(n) - L(n)`
computable by an FPT algorithm parameterized by its own value?

---

## 1. "Distance to bound" parameterization is a studied framework

**[literature]** The parameterization of optimization problems by the *excess
over a computable lower bound* is a well-established paradigm in parameterized
complexity, known as **"parameterization above guarantee"** (additive form).

- **Primary source:** Mahajan and Raman (2009) introduced the framework for
  Max Cut and Max SAT, parameterizing by the excess over a tight lower bound
  (e.g., `ceil(m/2)` for Max Cut). The key insight: when the standard
  parameter (solution size) is large relative to the input, parameterizing by
  the distance from a tight bound is more meaningful.
  
- **Comprehensive survey:** Gutin and Mnich, *"A Survey on Graph Problems
  Parameterized Above and Below Guaranteed Values"*, Computer Science Review
  (2024), arXiv:2207.12278. This survey covers additive and multiplicative
  above-guarantee parameterizations for Vertex Cover, Max Cut, TSP, Longest
  Path, and many other problems.

- **Key technique:** For a problem with tight lower bound `g(I)` computable in
  polynomial time, the above-guarantee parameterization asks whether
  `OPT(I) <= g(I) + k` (or `>= g(I) + k` for maximization). The goal is an
  algorithm running in `f(k)*poly(|I|)` time.

**[deduction]** Our problem is exactly an above-guarantee parameterization:
- The "problem" is computing `S(n)` (the optimal comparison-tree height).
- The "guarantee" is `L(n) = ceil(log2 n!)`, a tight lower bound computable in
  polynomial time via Stirling's approximation.
- The "parameter" is `s = k - L(n)`, the excess over the information bound.
- The question is whether `S(n) <= L(n) + s` is decidable in `f(s)*poly(n)`.

This is the **first application of the above-guarantee framework to sorting
numbers** `[conjecture]` — the literature does not appear to contain this
specific application.

## 2. If `e(n)` is {0,1}-valued for all `n`, FPT reduces to deciding `S(n) = L(n)`

**[deduction]** Suppose `e(n) in {0,1}` for all `n`. Then:

| Parameter `s` | Answer | Time |
|---------------|--------|------|
| `s < 0` | NO (information bound violated) | `O(1)` |
| `s = 0` | YES iff `e(n) = 0`, i.e., `S(n) = L(n)` | `poly(n)` if decidable |
| `s >= 1` | YES (since `e(n) <= 1 <= s`) | `O(1)` |

Thus the problem is FPT **if and only if** deciding `S(n) = L(n)` is in
`poly(n)` time. This is the famous open problem of whether the information-
theoretic bound is tight.

**[open]** Whether `S(n) = L(n)` is decidable in polynomial time is a major
open problem in sorting theory. No such algorithm is known. The best exact
algorithms run in `2^{O(n log^2 n)}` time (budgeted DFS, see
`docs/research-budgeted-dfs.md`).

**[deduction]** Therefore, **if** `e(n)` is {0,1}-valued for all `n`, the FPT
question for the surplus parameterization is **equivalent to the information-
bound tightness problem**. This is a precise reduction: FPT in `s` implies
poly-time decidability of `S(n) = L(n)` (by setting `s = 0`), and conversely.

## 3. Is `e(n)` plausibly {0,1}-valued? (Falsification attempt)

**[literature]** Known values of `e(n) = S(n) - L(n)` for `n <= 22`:

| n | L(n) | S(n) | e(n) |
|---|------|------|------|
| 1-11 | 0,1,3,5,7,10,13,16,19,22,26 | same | 0 |
| 12 | 29 | 30 | 1 |
| 13 | 33 | 34 | 1 |
| 14 | 37 | 38 | 1 |
| 15 | 41 | 42 | 1 |
| 16 | 45 | 46 | 1 |
| 17 | 49 | 50 | 1 |
| 18 | 53 | 54 | 1 |
| 19 | 57 | 58 | 1 |
| 20 | 62 | 62 | 0 |
| 21 | 66 | 66 | 0 |
| 22 | 70 | 71 | 1 |

Sources: Wells (1965) for `S(12)=30`; Kasai et al. (1994) for `S(13)=34`;
Peczarski (2002, 2004) for `S(14), S(15), S(22)`; Stober and Weiss (2022,
arXiv:2206.05597) for `S(16), S(17), S(18)`.

**[observation]** `e(n)` is non-monotone: `e(12)=1`, `e(20)=0`, `e(22)=1`.
This rules out any simple "threshold" structure.

**[open]** Whether `e(n) in {0,1}` for all `n` is a famous open problem. It is
also open whether `e(n)` is unbounded (i.e., `e(n) -> infinity`).

**[conjecture]** The non-monotonicity and lack of a known recurrence for `e(n)`
suggest that `e(n)` may NOT be {0,1}-valued for all `n`. The structural
obstruction at `n=12` (where the information bound first fails) appears to be
a global/geometric phenomenon rather than a local one, and similar obstructions
could produce larger excesses at larger `n`.

---

## 4. Non-monotonicity and transfer obstructions

**[deduction]** The non-monotonicity of `e(n)` is a **structural obstruction**
to naive FPT approaches:

1. **No monotone recurrence:** If `e(n)` were monotone non-decreasing, one
   could compute `e(n)` incrementally. But `e(20)=0 < e(22)=1` while
   `e(12)=1 > e(11)=0` shows no monotonicity.

2. **No across-n recurrence:** The minimax recurrence for `S(n)` operates on
   posets of size `n`, and the structure of optimal decision trees for `n`
   elements does not appear to decompose into optimal trees for smaller `n`
   in a way that preserves the excess over `L(n)`. This is the "across-n
   recurrence non-exactness" already noted on #301.

3. **Transfer obstruction:** Even if `e(m)` were known for all `m < n`, there
   is no known way to compute `e(n)` from these values. The excess depends on
   the global geometry of the poset of reachable states, not on a simple
   recurrence.

**[deduction]** These obstructions mean that **standard FPT techniques
(kernels, bounded search trees, color coding) do not apply directly** to the
surplus parameterization. The above-guarantee literature succeeds by
exploiting problem-specific structure (e.g., LP relaxations for Vertex Cover,
separator structure for Max Cut). No such structure is known for sorting
numbers.

---

## 5. Distinguishing tree/algorithm size from runtime

**[deduction]** It is important to distinguish:

- **Lower bound on tree/state size:** The number of posets reachable within
  `S(n)` comparisons is at most `2^{O(n log^2 n)}` (budgeted DFS). This is a
  statement about the *search space size*, not the algorithm runtime.

- **Lower bound on algorithm runtime:** No superpolynomial lower bound is
  known for computing `S(n)`. The problem could be in `P`, in which case the
  surplus parameterization is trivially FPT.

**[open]** Whether any superpolynomial lower bound exists for computing `S(n)`
is open. The problem is not known to be NP-hard, and Mahaney's theorem blocks
ordinary NP-hardness for the sparse unary language.

---

## 6. Consequence for FPT

**[deduction]** The FPT status of the surplus parameterization is at least as
hard as deciding `S(n) = L(n)`:

- **If `e(n)` is {0,1}-valued:** FPT iff `S(n) = L(n)` is in `poly(n)`.
  This is the information-bound tightness problem, a famous open problem.

- **If `e(n)` is unbounded:** The problem may or may not be FPT. The
  above-guarantee framework does not automatically imply FPT — it depends on
  whether the excess structure admits efficient algorithms. No such structure
  is known for sorting numbers.

- **If `e(n)` is bounded (but not necessarily {0,1}-valued):** Say
  `e(n) <= C` for all `n`. Then for `s >= C`, the answer is always YES; for
  `s < C`, there are only finitely many cases. The problem is FPT iff each
  of these finitely many cases is decidable in `poly(n)` time.

**[conjecture]** The surplus parameterization is **not FPT** unless a major
breakthrough occurs in understanding the structure of `e(n)`. The
non-monotonicity, lack of recurrence, and global nature of the obstruction at
`n=12` all point to deep structural difficulty.

---

## 7. Next step

**[open]** The decisive next step is to determine whether `e(n)` can exceed 1.
A promising approach:

1. **Computational:** Extend the budgeted DFS to `n=23` or beyond. The value
   `S(23)` is known to be 75 or 76 (information bound: 75). If `S(23)=76`,
   then `e(23)=1`, consistent with {0,1}-valued. If `S(23)=75`, then
   `e(23)=0`. Neither outcome resolves the question for all `n`.

2. **Theoretical:** Study the structure of the obstruction at `n=12` (and
   `n=13,...,19,22`) to understand whether it can produce excess >= 2 at
   larger `n`. This requires new combinatorial invariants beyond leaf counting.

3. **Parameterized:** Investigate whether the above-guarantee framework can
   be adapted to exploit any known structure of optimal sorting trees (e.g.,
   the Ford-Johnson construction) to obtain FPT algorithms for restricted
   parameter ranges.
